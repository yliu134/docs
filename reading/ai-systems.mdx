The AI systems track runs in parallel with the core systems fundamentals throughout the entire eight months. Months 1–3 are intentionally light — about 45 minutes per week — so the systems fundamentals get the depth they need. Months 4–6 scale up as you begin a shippable project that becomes your primary portfolio artifact. The reading list below is organized accordingly: start with the foundational primer and the MCP spec in Month 1, add one paper per month from Month 2 onwards, and let the blog sources run continuously as a low-volume subscription throughout the plan.

---

## Foundational Reading

Start here on Day 1. Both of these are short enough to read in a single session.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Building Effective Agents" icon="book">
    **Anthropic Engineering · anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents**

    The honest primer on agent design. Anthropic's team covers the architectural patterns that actually work in production — augmented LLMs, prompt chaining, routing, parallelization, orchestrator-subagent structures, and full autonomous agents — with explicit guidance on when each pattern is appropriate and when you're over-engineering. Read this before you write a single line of agent code.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The Model Context Protocol Specification" icon="file-code">
    **Anthropic · modelcontextprotocol.io**

    MCP is the emerging standard for how LLM applications connect to external tools, data sources, and services. Read the spec end-to-end as you would any protocol specification — understand the transport layer, the message format, the capability negotiation, and the security model. This is the protocol your AI systems project will almost certainly use.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Papers

<Note>
  One paper per month. Read every paper adversarially: abstract and introduction first, then ask your AI to challenge you on the claims before you read the methodology and results. Two adversarial exchanges per paper is the minimum — one before reading, one after.
</Note>

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Attention Is All You Need" icon="file-lines">
    **Vaswani et al. · 2017**

    The transformer paper. You don't need to implement it — you need the mental model: why attention replaced recurrence, what multi-head attention is doing, and how the encoder-decoder architecture works. Read it for the conceptual foundation, not the math. Once you have this, every explanation of how LLMs work will land properly.
  </Card>
  <Card title="ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in LLMs" icon="file-lines">
    **Yao et al. · 2022**

    The paper that formalized the Reason + Act loop that most agent frameworks implement. ReAct interleaves reasoning traces with action steps, allowing the model to plan, execute tool calls, observe results, and adjust. Read this before you build any agent — it explains the pattern you're implementing and its failure modes.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools" icon="file-lines">
    **Schick et al. · Meta AI · 2023**

    Toolformer shows how models can learn to call external APIs by self-supervising on when and how to use tools. Read this after ReAct — it gives you a different perspective on tool use as a learned capability rather than a prompted behavior. Relevant for evaluating when fine-tuning vs prompting is the right approach for tool-calling.
  </Card>
  <Card title="One RAG or Agent-Eval Survey per Quarter" icon="file-lines">
    **Various · Ongoing**

    The RAG and agent evaluation literature moves quickly. Each quarter, find one recent survey paper on retrieval-augmented generation or agent evaluation (RAGAS, ARES, or similar) and read it adversarially. Use your AI to find the most-cited survey from the last six months — the field is evolving fast enough that last year's survey may already be outdated.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Engineering Blogs

<Tip>
  These blogs run as a continuous subscription throughout all eight months — one or two posts per week from whichever author has published recently. For vocabulary and taste, not just information. A post you can't summarize back in five sentences was not read.
</Tip>

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Individual Engineers">
    | Blog | Focus |
    |------|-------|
    | **Simon Willison** (simonwillison.net) | Best-in-class practical AI engineering. Willison covers LLM capabilities, tool use, and the engineering decisions behind real AI products with more clarity and less hype than anyone else writing publicly. Read everything he publishes. |
    | **Eugene Yan** (eugeneyan.com) | ML system design writeups. Yan covers the engineering side of deploying and evaluating ML systems — retrieval, reranking, evaluation pipelines, and the operational challenges of running models in production. More systems-focused than most AI blogs. |
    | **Chip Huyen** (huyenchip.com) | LLMs in production. Huyen covers the infrastructure and systems challenges of running large language models at scale — serving, latency, cost optimization, and the organizational challenges of ML in production. Her writing bridges the gap between ML research and systems engineering. |
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Company Blogs">
    | Blog | Focus |
    |------|-------|
    | **Anthropic Engineering Blog** | Deep technical posts on Claude's capabilities, alignment research, and the engineering challenges of building safe and capable AI systems. Read the engineering posts; the alignment research is interesting but lower priority for an AI systems engineer. |
    | **OpenAI Cookbook** (cookbook.openai.com) | Practical implementation guides for common patterns — RAG, tool use, structured outputs, embeddings. Use this as a reference when building your project, not as a reading list. |
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Talks and Podcasts

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="AI Engineer Summit" icon="play">
    **YouTube · Various Speakers**
    Search "AI Engineer Summit" on YouTube to find the free recordings.

    The best conference for engineers building AI systems (as opposed to researchers building AI models). The recordings are free on YouTube. Watch 1–2 talks per month from Month 1–3, and increase to 2–3 per month when your AI project starts. Prioritize talks on agent architecture, evaluation, and observability.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Latent Space Podcast" icon="headphones">
    **Swyx & Alessio · latent.space**

    The best podcast for keeping current with AI engineering without spending hours per week on it. 1–2 episodes per month is the right budget. Listen to episodes that match your current project focus — skip episodes that are pure research or pure product unless they're directly relevant to your work.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Project Candidates

<Note>
  Choose one project at the start of Month 4. The project is your primary portfolio artifact — it needs to be deployed, observable, and something you can walk an interviewer through end-to-end.
</Note>

<Accordion title="Agent Eval + Observability Platform">
  Build a platform for tracing, replaying, and regression-testing agent runs. Captures traces of agent execution (tool calls, reasoning steps, responses), allows you to replay a run with a modified prompt or model, and runs regression tests against a golden set of inputs. This project directly combines Month 5 observability work with AI systems engineering. High signal for senior roles that care about production ML.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Coding/Debugging Assistant with Retrieval and Memory">
  A coding assistant that uses RAG over a codebase, maintains a memory of past interactions, and can execute debugging steps via tool calls. Requires an embedding pipeline, a retrieval layer, and a memory store — all systems problems. Good for demonstrating that you can build end-to-end AI systems, not just prompt wrapper applications.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Agent Workflow Orchestrator">
  A durable execution framework for multi-step agent workflows with retries, timeouts, and human-in-the-loop checkpoints. Directly applies distributed systems fundamentals — you'll need to think about exactly-once execution, state persistence, and failure handling in the same way you think about Kafka consumers. High ceiling for systems engineers who want to demonstrate depth.
</Accordion>
