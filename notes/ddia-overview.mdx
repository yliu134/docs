*Designing Data-Intensive Applications* by Martin Kleppmann opens with the three properties that every data system must balance: reliability, scalability, and maintainability. Every subsequent topic in the systems track — storage engines, replication, distributed transactions, consensus — builds on the vocabulary and governing trade-offs that Chapter 1 introduces. The chapter reads fast, but it earns its place: the three words in the title are not marketing copy, they are measurable properties, and most engineering debates reduce to a disagreement about which one to prioritize.

## The Three Properties

<CardGroup cols={3}>
  <Card title="Reliability" icon="shield-check">
    The system continues to work correctly — at the right level of performance — even when things go wrong. Faults are expected; failures are what you prevent.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Scalability" icon="chart-line">
    As the system grows in data volume, traffic, or complexity, there are reasonable ways to deal with that growth. Growth must be described before it can be managed.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Maintainability" icon="wrench">
    Over time, many different people can work on the system productively. Operability, simplicity, and evolvability are the three sub-properties.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Reliability: Fault vs. Failure

The most important distinction in this chapter is also the one most frequently blurred in conversation: a **fault** is one component deviating from spec; a **failure** is the whole system stopping to provide the required service. Your goal is to build fault-tolerant systems — systems that survive faults without becoming failures.

<Note>
  Tolerating faults is not the same as preventing them. You cannot prevent a hard drive from dying, a cosmic ray from flipping a bit, or an operator from running the wrong migration. Fault tolerance means the system degrades gracefully when those inevitable events occur.
</Note>

DDIA distinguishes three categories of fault:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Hardware Faults">
    Disk failures, RAM errors, power outages, network card failures. Historically handled by redundancy — RAID arrays, dual power supplies, hot-standby servers. At large enough scale, hardware failures become a daily routine, not an exceptional event.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Software Errors">
    Systematic faults that tend to cause many simultaneous failures: bugs triggered by unusual input, runaway processes consuming shared resources, cascading failures across services. Harder to handle than hardware faults because they violate the assumption that nodes fail independently.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Human Errors">
    Misconfiguration is the leading cause of outages, not hardware failures. Mitigations include well-designed abstractions that make the right action easy, sandbox environments that decouple experimentation from production, and fast rollback paths.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Scalability: Describing Load

You cannot reason about scaling without first describing load precisely. DDIA introduces **load parameters** as the tool for that description — the numbers that characterize your system's workload.

The right load parameters depend on the system. For a web server, it might be requests per second. For a database, it might be the ratio of reads to writes. For Twitter's fan-out problem — the example DDIA uses — it's the distribution of followers per user, because that single number determines whether broadcasting a tweet is cheap (write once, read many) or expensive (write to every follower's timeline on post).

<Tip>
  Choosing the wrong load parameter leads to the wrong scaling strategy. If your p99 latency is caused by a small number of users with very large fan-out, scaling horizontally without addressing that tail achieves nothing. Describe the skew before designing the solution.
</Tip>

### Percentile Latency: Why p99 Beats the Mean

Once you can describe load, you describe performance under load. DDIA makes a strong case that **response time percentiles** are the correct unit — not averages.

The mean response time tells you very little because it averages together fast and slow requests. A single 10-second request averaged with 999 ten-millisecond requests looks like 20 ms average — but one user in a thousand had a terrible experience.

<Accordion title="Why percentiles matter: the concrete case">
  Suppose you have these response times across 1,000 requests:
  - 950 requests completed in 10 ms → p50 ≈ 10 ms
  - 40 requests took 100 ms
  - 9 requests took 1,000 ms
  - 1 request took 10,000 ms

  **Mean:** roughly 30 ms — looks fine.  
  **p99:** 1,000 ms — one in a hundred users waits a full second.  
  **p99.9:** 10,000 ms — the worst user waits ten seconds.

  In a checkout flow with SLA targets, p99 is the contractual number. The mean is irrelevant.
</Accordion>

The practical notation: **p50** is the median, **p95/p99/p999** are the tail. High percentiles (p99, p999) are called **tail latencies**.

### Tail Latency Amplification in Fan-Out

Tail latency compounds dangerously in architectures where a single user-facing request fans out to multiple backend calls. If a page requires 100 backend requests, the probability that at least one of them hits p99 latency approaches certainty — even if each individual request hits p99 only 1% of the time.

<Warning>
  In a parallel fan-out to *n* services, the slowest response sets the total latency. With n=100 services each at 99% fast, the probability of *no* slow call is 0.99^100 ≈ 37%. You're more likely than not to experience tail latency on every single request.
</Warning>

This is why optimizing tail latency specifically — not just average latency — matters for any architecture that aggregates results from multiple downstream services.

## Maintainability: Three Sub-Properties

Maintainability is about the people who build and operate the system after you. DDIA breaks it into three:

<CardGroup cols={3}>
  <Card title="Operability" icon="gears">
    Make it easy for operations teams to keep the system running. Good monitoring, predictable behavior, documented failure modes, easy rollback.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Simplicity" icon="cube">
    Remove accidental complexity — complexity that comes from implementation choices, not the problem itself. Good abstractions hide implementation details without leaking them.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Evolvability" icon="arrows-rotate">
    Make it easy to change the system in the future. Also called extensibility or plasticity. Closely tied to Chapter 4 (encoding and schema evolution).
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Key Terms to Internalize

Chapter 1 introduces vocabulary that every later chapter assumes. Being able to explain these terms precisely — not just name them — is the signal that the chapter has done its work:

<Accordion title="Core terms and what precision looks like">
  **Fault tolerance:** Surviving component failures without becoming a system failure. Achieved through redundancy, timeouts, and graceful degradation — not by making individual components perfect.

  **Load parameter:** A number that describes the shape of your workload (e.g., requests/sec, read/write ratio, fan-out distribution). Picking the wrong load parameter leads to the wrong scaling strategy.

  **p99 latency:** 99% of requests complete faster than this value. More useful than mean latency because it captures the tail experience, and tail latency amplifies in fan-out architectures.

  **Accidental complexity:** Kleppmann borrows this from Fred Brooks — complexity that the system carries because of implementation choices, not because the problem itself demands it.
</Accordion>
