Most self-study plans drift because there is no forcing function between "I started" and "I got the job." The Engineering Growth framework solves that with two hard checkpoints: one at Month 3 that keeps you ready to interview at any time, and one at Month 6 that marks the formal transition to active searching. Hit both and Months 7–8 become execution, not catch-up.

---

## Month 3 — Always-Interview-Ready

By the end of Month 3, you should be able to step into an interview loop on short notice without scrambling. This checkpoint is not about being fully prepared — it is about being warm, current, and presentable.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Update your resume and LinkedIn">
    Reflect the work you have done in Months 1–3. List the artifacts you have produced, the systems you have gone deep on, and the depth bar you have hit. Concrete over vague — "reproduced a gap-lock deadlock end-to-end under InnoDB REPEATABLE READ" is stronger than "studied MySQL internals."
  </Step>
  <Step title="Hold an unbroken artifact cadence for the last 4 weeks">
    The weekly artifact habit is the core of the plan. At Month 3, verify that the last four Sundays each have an artifact recorded in your review. A broken cadence at this checkpoint means the habit is not yet load-bearing — fix it before Month 4 starts.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Complete at least 1 system-design writeup">
    A full written design answer — stated requirements, design, trade-offs, failure modes — done to interview shape. Not published, not shared: rehearsed. One is the minimum; two is better.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Keep your algo muscle warm">
    The last four weeks of algorithm problems are complete. Two per week, every week. Warmth here means you can sit down with a LeetCode medium and think clearly, not that you have memorized patterns.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
  Passing this checkpoint does not mean you should start applying. It means that if a strong referral surfaces in Month 4, you do not have to say "give me three more months." You say yes, and you are ready.
</Note>

## Month 6 — Job-Search-Ready

The Month 6 checkpoint is the formal green light to start recruiting conversations. Everything from Months 1–5 has been building toward this moment.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Hold your artifact cadence — with 3–5 standout pieces">
    The weekly habit should be unbroken across the full six months. More importantly, you should be able to identify three to five artifacts strong enough to walk a hiring manager through in a conversation — pieces that demonstrate real depth, not just familiarity.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Deploy your Track B project, fully observable">
    Your Track B project is running, instrumented (OTEL, traces, metrics), and something you can demo. This is the one deliverable a hiring manager will actually ask about. If it is not deployed and observable by Month 6, it is not ready for a resume line.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Start recruiter conversations">
    Reach out to recruiters at target companies. You do not need to be actively interviewing yet — but the conversation pipeline should be open. Hiring timelines can stretch 6–8 weeks, so starting at Month 6 puts first-round screens in Month 7.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Draft your referral list">
    List every person who could refer you at a target company. Prioritize warm connections — former colleagues, collaborators, people who have seen you work. Referral-driven screens move faster and convert better than cold applications.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
  Referral-driven screens are the primary path to interviews at most senior backend roles. Your artifacts help you **pass** those interviews — they do comparatively little to help you **get** them. Build the referral list with the same care you give your artifact cadence.
</Tip>

## Month 7–8 — Active Interviewing

Months 7–8 are execution months. Your systems knowledge is deep, your Track B project is live, and your artifact library gives you concrete answers to "tell me about a hard technical problem you've worked through."

During this period:
- Run one mock interview per week rather than per month
- Keep the algorithm cadence at two problems per week — do not let it lapse under scheduling pressure
- Use your system-design writeups as active rehearsal material, not just notes
- Track every interview loop: what round you are in, what signals you are getting, what you want to improve

<Warning>
  Do not cut system-design prep to make time for more algorithm practice in Months 7–8. System design is the round that most often decides a senior backend loop — and the slowest skill to rebuild if you let it go cold.
</Warning>

## Monthly 1:1 With Your Mentor

Every month, you meet with your mentor and walk them through that month's artifact. This is a standing commitment, not an optional check-in.

**What to prepare:**

<Accordion title="Choose the right artifact">
  Pick the artifact that most surprised you — the one where you thought you understood something and then discovered you didn't. That is the piece that generates the best conversation and reveals the most about what you actually know versus what you think you know.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Walk through the mechanism, not the summary">
  Do not present a summary of what you read. Walk through the mechanism at the depth bar: state what it does, what problem it was introduced to solve, what it cost, and what you'd have gotten wrong before studying it. The "my miss" line from the five-line reduction is the centerpiece.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Come with a question you cannot yet answer">
  Mentors are most useful when you have a genuine gap you cannot resolve alone. Come with one specific open question — something you marked as unverified in your notes, or a trade-off you can see both sides of and cannot resolve. That is the productive surface for a 1:1.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Report honestly on the cadence">
  If an artifact week was missed or a track fell behind, say so. The rules are explicit: miss a day, drop it; miss a week, Sunday resets, no catch-up. Your mentor is not a judge — they are a thinking partner. Honest accounting leads to better advice than a polished performance.
</Accordion>
